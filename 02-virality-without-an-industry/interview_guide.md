# Semi-Structured Interview Guide

**Study:** Virality Without an Industry? The Political Economy of Pakistan's Music Revival, 2014-2026  
**Version:** 1.0  
**Date:** 23 August 2026  
**Primary interview length:** 60-75 minutes  
**Languages:** English, Urdu, Punjabi, or another language chosen by the participant. Use a qualified interpreter where the research team lacks fluency.

## 1. Purpose

The interviews examine how Pakistan's music economy changed between 2014 and 2026, with particular attention to entry costs, platform distribution, income, contracts, rights, corporate sponsorship, live performance, social inequality, and international circulation. Interviews are evidence about mechanisms and lived experience. They are not a substitute for audited financial records or a representative labour-force survey.

## 2. Target sample

| Participant group | Target | Selection principle |
|---|---:|---|
| Artists and recurring acts | 12 | Vary genre, language, gender, class background, city/region, career stage, and institutional support |
| Producers, engineers, composers | 5 | Include home, commercial-studio, branded-show, and film/television pathways |
| Managers, labels, distributors, publishers | 5 | Include local and international-facing intermediaries |
| Brands, platforms, programme curators | 3 | Include production, marketing, editorial, or partnership responsibility |
| Venues, promoters, festival or event organizers | 3 | Include commercial and independent live circuits |
| Copyright, policy, and rights specialists | 3 | Include legal practice, collective management, and government/policy knowledge |
| Journalists, critics, radio or archive workers | 3 | Include historical and regional coverage |
| Audience and fan-community participants | 4 | Vary geography, age, language, and platform habits |
| **Total target** | **38** | Purposive maximum-variation sample, not a population sample |

Use snowball referrals only to fill a defined sampling cell. Do not allow one prominent network to become the entire sample merely because famous people tend to know other famous people.

## 3. Pre-interview protocol

1. Assign an interview ID before contact.
2. Record the sampling reason, recruitment route, conflicts of interest, and whether the participant has authority to speak for an organization.
3. Send the information sheet at least 24 hours before the interview when practical.
4. Ask separately for permission to record, quote by name, quote anonymously, and retain documents.
5. Offer a non-recorded interview or written response where recording creates risk.
6. Do not request confidential contracts as a condition of participation.

## 4. Consent script

> This research studies changes in Pakistan's music economy and popular culture since 2014. Participation is voluntary. You may decline any question, pause, or stop the interview. With your permission, I would like to record the conversation for transcription. You can choose to be named, described only by role, or kept anonymous. We will not publish private financial figures, contracts, or identifying details without your explicit permission. Before using a sensitive quotation, we can return it to you for verification. Do you consent to participate, and do you consent to recording?

Record the answers separately. Consent to participate does not imply consent to recording or identification.

## 5. Core interview modules

### A. Career pathway and historical change

1. How did you first enter music, and what resources made entry possible?
2. What changed most in your work between 2014 and today?
3. Which two or three events most affected your opportunities: mobile internet, YouTube's return, local streaming services, Spotify, short-form video, the pandemic, branded programmes, political restrictions, or something else?
4. What remained stubbornly unchanged?

**Probe:** equipment, training, family support, city, language, class background, unpaid time, mentors, diaspora ties, and professional networks.

### B. Production and costs

5. Walk me through the production of a typical track from idea to release.
6. Who pays for composition, recording, mixing, mastering, video, artwork, distribution, publicity, and live rehearsal?
7. Which costs have fallen since 2014, and which have risen?
8. What work is unpaid, underpaid, exchanged as a favour, or financed by another job?

**Optional document request:** anonymized budget, invoice, studio rate sheet, or royalty statement.

### C. Income, precarity, and payment

9. What are your current sources of music-related income?
10. What share of your total personal income came from music in the last 12 months?

Use bands unless the participant volunteers an exact figure:

- Less than 10 percent
- 10-25 percent
- 26-50 percent
- 51-75 percent
- More than 75 percent
- Prefer not to answer

11. Which source is most reliable: live fees, brand work, streaming, teaching, production, film/television, sync licensing, patronage, merchandise, or another source?
12. How often are payments delayed, disputed, reduced, or never received?
13. Could you continue this career for the next three years without income from outside music or family support?

Do not convert income bands into exact rupee estimates after the interview. Humans have already invented enough fictitious precision.

### D. Contracts, ownership, and rights

14. Who normally owns the master recording and composition rights?
15. Are agreements written, verbal, platform-standard, or negotiated through a representative?
16. Which clauses are most difficult to understand or negotiate?
17. How are royalties, neighbouring rights, performance rights, and sync fees tracked and paid?
18. Have you experienced unauthorized use, takedown disputes, missing metadata, or credit errors?
19. What would effective copyright administration look like in practice?

### E. Platforms and discovery

20. Which platforms matter at release, after release, and for long-term catalogue discovery?
21. How do playlists, recommendation systems, short-form trends, paid promotion, and social relationships affect visibility?
22. Have you changed song length, release frequency, openings, language, metadata, or video strategy because of platform incentives?
23. What platform analytics can you access, and which important information remains unavailable?
24. Describe a release that performed unexpectedly well and one that deserved more attention but did not receive it.

### F. Corporate sponsorship and institutional selection

25. What do branded programmes provide that independent or label pathways do not?
26. How are artists selected, and who has influence over repertoire, language, appearance, credits, or political content?
27. What rights and payments remain with the artist after participation?
28. Does branded visibility produce sustained work after the campaign ends?
29. What kinds of music or artists are difficult for brands to support?

### G. Live music and local infrastructure

30. How important are concerts, weddings, festivals, universities, clubs, cultural centres, religious events, and private functions to income and discovery?
31. What limits the live sector: venue supply, licensing, security, gender norms, sponsorship, ticketing, inflation, travel, or censorship?
32. How do opportunities differ among Karachi, Lahore, Islamabad/Rawalpindi, Peshawar, Quetta, secondary cities, and rural areas?

### H. Class, gender, language, and region

33. Who can afford to remain in music long enough to become visible?
34. How do safety, mobility, family permission, harassment, dress, and respectability affect participation?
35. Are artists from regional or working-class backgrounds credited and paid differently once their music reaches national platforms?
36. Which languages are rewarded by domestic platforms, brands, diaspora listeners, and Indian audiences?
37. Has representation improved without equivalent ownership or decision power?

Do not ask participants from marginalized communities to explain an entire group. Ask about their own experience and the structures they observe.

### I. Internationalization and geopolitics

38. Where are your most important audiences and collaborators located?
39. What roles do India, the Gulf, Europe, North America, and the Pakistani diaspora play in reach and revenue?
40. How do visas, payment systems, platform geography, political tensions, and content restrictions affect collaboration?
41. Does international recognition create opportunities for the wider domestic ecosystem, or mainly for individual artists?

### J. Regulation, censorship, and self-censorship

42. Have platform blocks, internet disruptions, content laws, broadcast rules, venue permissions, or informal pressure changed what you release?
43. Which risks are legal, commercial, familial, religious, or political?
44. What subjects or identities are underrepresented because the cost of expression is too high?

### K. Definition of an industry and future

45. What would need to exist before you would call Pakistan's music scene a functioning industry?
46. Which institution is most urgently missing: rights collection, labels, financing, venues, training, data, unions, export support, or something else?
47. Who currently captures the greatest value from a successful track?
48. What single policy or market change would most improve sustainable careers?
49. What evidence would convince you that conditions had genuinely improved?

## 6. Role-specific probes

### Artists

- Describe the route from first upload to first paid opportunity.
- Who owns masters and publishing for your three most important releases?
- What work do you do between releases to remain visible?
- What opportunity did you decline, and why?

### Producers and engineers

- How are studio and production fees set?
- Are producers paid upfront, through points, through royalties, or not at all?
- How often are credits and metadata corrected after release?
- Has home production expanded the market or depressed professional rates?

### Labels, managers, distributors, and publishers

- How do you evaluate and finance a release?
- What is recoupable, and how are statements issued?
- Which revenue sources are material in Pakistan?
- What prevents catalogue acquisition, publishing administration, and export growth?

### Brands, platforms, and curators

- What are the formal selection criteria?
- Which performance indicators define campaign success?
- What data are shared with artists?
- How are rights, reuse, takedowns, and archive access governed after a season or campaign?

### Live-sector actors

- What is the economics of a typical event?
- How are ticket, sponsor, artist, security, and venue risks allocated?
- Which cities and genres remain commercially viable?
- How have inflation and public-order restrictions changed programming?

### Lawyers, policy, and rights specialists

- Which rights exist in statute but fail in practice?
- How are ownership and assignment disputes resolved?
- What collective-management or metadata systems operate, and with what coverage?
- What happened after the 2023 national music-policy process?

### Journalists, critics, radio, and archivists

- Which genres and regions are systematically underdocumented?
- How has music journalism's business model changed?
- What archives or charts can support a release census?
- Where do press narratives confuse virality with industrial growth?

### Audience and fan communities

- How do you discover, share, save, and pay for Pakistani music?
- Which platforms, languages, and genres dominate your listening?
- Does a viral sound lead you to follow the artist or only reuse the clip?
- What makes music feel local, national, regional, or global?

## 7. Closing

1. What have I not asked that is necessary to understand the industry?
2. Who holds a different view and should be interviewed?
3. May the research team contact you to verify a quotation or factual detail?
4. May de-identified observations from this interview be archived for future research?

After the interview, complete a reflexive memo within 24 hours recording the setting, power dynamics, contradictions, documents promised, sampling relevance, and any risk that requires follow-up.
