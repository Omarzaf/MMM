Virality Without an Industry?

The Political Economy of Pakistan's Music Revival, 2014–2026

A research design, not a victory lap.

00 Opening contradiction

Pakistani music now reaches audiences through channels that barely existed in 2014, when this study begins. That reach is real. But reach alone does not prove that musicians have secure work, clear rights over their songs, or an industry that can track who gets paid.

That gap matters because the public record is loudest about the things that are easiest to see. It can date the 2014 jump to mobile internet, YouTube’s return in 2016, Spotify’s arrival in 2021, and the later moments when Pakistani songs caught global attention. It also shows that none of these channels came with public records of royalties, contracts, working conditions, or who owns what. Side A — Circulation

01 Infrastructure in flux

The study starts in 2014 because access to the internet changed first, before anyone could say that Pakistani music had moved online. That year, Pakistan auctioned licences for 3G and 4G mobile networks. The auction is a clean starting point: a dated public event that marks the shift from limited mobile internet to mass mobile broadband. What matters is less the sale itself than what came after. The government’s Economic Survey shows broadband subscriptions rising from 16,885,518 in 2014–15 to 102,699,967 in 2020–21. Those are subscriptions, not individual people, listeners, artists, or paying music fans. Still, they show why sharing music online suddenly became a realistic idea.

Sources S002 L001

The middle of the decade brought a second opening. YouTube returned in January 2016 after being blocked for several years, this time through a local Pakistani version of the site. That return should not be romanticized as a move from censorship to freedom. It reopened a powerful route for music, but the state kept the power to restrict platforms again. For musicians and labels, the practical change was simple: a video site that almost everyone used was back, for releasing songs, being discovered, storing work, and being searched for. For this research, the limit matters just as much. The return proves that a channel was open. It does not prove income, an equal chance of being recommended, or reliable payment.

Sources S004 L004 L005

That limit keeps this chapter from becoming a tidy cause-and-effect story. Broadband growth and YouTube’s return came before the famous moments when Pakistani songs went global, but coming first is not the same as causing. The public record supports a timeline of conditions that made things possible: more mobile data subscriptions, a video platform back online, and a bigger potential audience. On their own, those facts cannot explain why one artist crossed over, why one playlist mattered, why one label deal happened, or whether an independent rapper in Karachi made a safer living. Better networks widened the roads that music could travel; they did not tell us who controlled the tollbooths.

Sources L001 S004 S025 S030

The years from 2021 to 2026 need the same caution. The current Economic Survey reports 119 million broadband subscriptions in 2021–22, 127 million in 2022–23, 139 million in 2023–24, 150 million in 2024–25, and 161 million by March 2026, when broadband penetration reached 64.2 percent. That is a major national record of connectivity, but it measures telecoms, not music. It counts subscriptions rather than individual people. It says nothing directly about how many people listen to music, how many pay for audio apps, or where in the country artists find opportunities. March 2026 is also only part of a year, so it cannot be compared with full years.

Sources L002

Total data use widens the picture, but not what we can conclude from it. The official table shows combined mobile and fixed-line data traffic rising from 16,250 petabytes (PB) in 2021–22 to an estimated 30,783 PB in 2025–26. The chart keeps that official total, but notes that the mobile and fixed figures for 2025–26 add up to 30,782 PB, one short of the reported total. More importantly, this is all internet traffic. It is not music, video, or audio streaming, and it is not evidence that artists earned more. The mismatch is tiny, but it is useful: even official statistics have flaws, and the chart should carry them.

Sources L002

The arrival of 5G in 2026 sits at the edge of the story for the same reason. A 5G spectrum auction and launches in a few selected cities mark the start of another round of network building. They do not mean nationwide use, and they have no proven effect on music. They matter because the ground is still shifting while this is being written. They do not give the 2014–2026 story a triumphant ending. The year is not over, and every chart treats 2026 as an epilogue of confirmed events rather than a full year of data.

Sources L020 L021 L002

Read this way, infrastructure is neither background scenery nor destiny. It is the floor that Pakistan’s online music visibility stands on. Hip-hop makes that floor easy to see, because rap crews, informal studios, discovery on phones, and self-released tracks all depend on channels that can be opened, blocked, promoted, priced, or ignored. But the floor is not the house. A better network can carry a song further while leaving open the questions of who manages the rights, who gets paid, how venues make money, and who has bargaining power.

Side A — Circulation

02 Distribution in stages

The first stage was not just global platforms arriving from outside. Before Spotify came to Pakistan, local start-ups tried to put Pakistani music catalogues online. Taazi and Patari were reported in 2015 as legal music-streaming services, and Patari’s early public story included catalogue access, an app strategy, talk of royalties, and later seed funding. Pakistani founders and musicians were already trying to build a lawful way to get paid for domestic music online. But launch stories are not enough. Founders’ plans, catalogue claims, and funding news do not prove that artists were reliably paid.

Sources L003 S015 S016 S042

Patari’s later payment disputes make that difference impossible to ignore. News reports described musicians complaining that they had not been paid, and the company struggling after its early promise. These reports are not audited accounts or a final verdict on every payment. What it shows is narrower but firmer: inside one local platform, building an audience, leaning on brand sponsors, struggling to sell subscriptions, and paying artists could all come apart. An app could make finding music feel modern while payment dates, royalty accounting, and artists’ bargaining power stayed fragile.

Sources S017 L007 S016

YouTube’s 2016 return sits beside these local experiments as a different kind of opening. No Pakistani music company had to build anything; the dominant video site simply became available again. Brand-funded shows also widened the mainstream route. Coke Studio and other corporate stages could offer polished production, huge audiences, and a showcase for Pakistani culture abroad, at a scale few independent artists could match. The trap is to read this as equal opportunity. A branded stage can lift a few chosen artists while the wider economy depends on a few middlemen, sponsors’ decisions, and undisclosed earnings after release.

Sources S004 S010 S018 S020 S021

Hip-hop shows this pattern more sharply. Rap crews and neighborhood networks could build an audience before any institution noticed them, and online channels made that word-of-mouth spread easier to see. Lyari Underground’s path ran from local collective work, to attention through Patari Tabeer, to disruption, and then to a studio its members built themselves. It shows visibility without the infrastructure problem being solved. Young Stunners’ public history points to a crew of friends, songs spread through file-sharing, and live shows before mainstream fame. These cases show distribution as a sequence of things people did, not just a platform logo.

Sources L024 L025 L030

The 2020 pandemic did not invent home recording, but it exposed how much it mattered. Live gigs, weddings, and venues were disrupted, while music made in bedrooms and home studios became more visible. Abdullah Siddiqui’s room-made path and reports on Coke Studio 2020 being produced in home studios show how remote production became part of the professional story. The point is not that every artist suddenly had equal tools. It is that music could keep circulating through bedrooms, laptops, phones, and long-distance collaboration while live income was under strain.

Sources L008 L009 L010 L032

Spotify’s arrival in 2021 added a global audio company and its playlist system. The launch was Spotify’s own announcement that it was now available in more than 80 new markets, including Pakistan. It gave no local subscriber numbers, no payout rates, and no promise of equal access to its editors’ playlists. Later Spotify Pakistan programs, including EQUAL, RADAR, Fresh Finds, masterclasses, and local playlists, show the company actively building a market. They are evidence of what Spotify did in Pakistan, not a neutral map of the whole music economy. Playlists and recommendation algorithms are gatekeepers that decide who gets heard, not empty pipes that simply deliver what listeners already want.

Sources S005 L014 S030 S031

Spotify’s five-year Pakistan figures are useful only at the right scale. Spotify reported that listening grew more than 750 percent since launch. Streams of Pakistani artists rose more than sevenfold since 2021, and the number of Pakistani artists on the platform rose nearly 75 percent. Users created more than 15 million playlists, and the average listener streamed more than 140 artists a year. These are Spotify’s own figures, given without the underlying totals and with nothing on revenue. They cannot be turned into a national market share, an average artist income, or proof that playlist spots were fairly shared across scenes.

Sources L019

The streaming years also brought deals with global record companies. Warner’s partnership with Giraffe, Mass Appeal’s partnerships with Pakistan-linked artists, and campaigns backed by platforms show Pakistan being plugged into the global industry, selectively. They show that Pakistani music, including music for Pakistanis abroad, caught the eye of global companies. They are limited because public announcements rarely reveal how a deal works: advances that must be earned back, who owns the recordings and the songs, how royalties are counted, or whether jobs were created. A global connection can spread music further while hiding who earns from it.

Sources S009 L015 S008 S010

So distribution unfolds in stages: local production, a boost from a platform or a brand, global circulation, and unanswered questions about rights and income. Hip-hop fits this sequence because its crews and bedroom producers often start outside the formal industry and reach platforms later. But the same sequence is a warning against overclaiming. Access widened faster than fair visibility, and visibility widened far faster than any public evidence of pay, ownership, or secure careers.

03 Evidence by design

The research is built so that different kinds of facts never get added up into a fake industry score. Official statistics describe national infrastructure: broadband subscriptions, how widespread they are, and total data traffic, each with dates and units. Platform figures describe activity inside one company’s own service. Small targeted surveys describe a handful of people chosen on purpose, not at random. Case studies trace the paths of particular artists, companies, policies, and scenes. Research targets name the data the study still plans to collect. Each kind can support a claim. None can quietly become the yardstick for another.

Sources L001 L002 L019 L022 S025

That is why the research plan keeps the current list of sources separate from future fieldwork. The package begins with 42 starter sources. The planned study targets a 360-track sample, with 93 track variables and 51 artist variables. Those numbers are not findings about Pakistan’s music industry. They are the size of the job: what has to be collected before the project can test claims about how songs are released, which get platform attention, who holds rights, how musicians work, and how mature the industry’s institutions are. Treating them as results would mistake preparation for proof.

The evidence-coverage grid is another map of what can be seen, not of how well things are going. A high score means a type of source says more about that question, not that conditions are good. Public sources say more about audience attention than about whether musicians can make a living. Interviews may be essential for income, contracts, and working conditions. Platform data may show chart positions or playlist spots without revealing who holds bargaining power. This is the whole argument in miniature: what can be seen is not always what matters most.

Sources S030 S031 S038 S039 S028

The livelihood snapshot uses a 2025 study by UNESCO and Beaconhouse National University (BNU) only as a small warning light. Its caption carries its limits: Purposive sample; n=50 music respondents; all respondents male; not nationally representative. In plain terms, the researchers picked respondents on purpose rather than at random, across 17 cities and six creative sectors. The music groups were small: 30 respondents from KP, Balochistan, and Gilgit-Baltistan; 10 from Sindh; and 10 from Punjab. That every respondent was a man is a gap in the survey, not evidence that women are absent from music work.

Sources L022 L023

The survey is still useful because it points to questions that platform data cannot answer. Some respondents were paid by the project; some clustered around very low monthly incomes; a smaller share reported earning above PKR 100,000 a month. Those figures should shape interview questions, contract reviews, and comparisons between regions. They are not an estimate of national wages. They sit beside platform and infrastructure evidence because they measure something different. Merging them into one growth number would make the page look more certain by making it less honest.

Sources L022 L023 S028

So Chapter 03 makes method part of the story. Pakistan’s music boom can be told through official statistics, platforms’ own claims, small surveys, case studies, and research targets, but each must keep its limits in view. Keeping them separate does not weaken the argument. It is what lets the argument say something precise. It has become easier to see how far music travels than how musicians live, and the next task is to follow the money to where the public record goes quiet.

Side B — Value

04 Propositions and tests

The second half of the argument, about value, begins by refusing the easiest story. A celebratory growth chart would look neat: broadband grows, platforms arrive, famous songs travel, and the line points up. It would also hide the hardest question. The evidence so far is strongest where music leaves a public trace, and weakest where we would need documents or interviews to see livelihoods, ownership, contracts, and payments. So instead of a growth chart, this section uses a ledger of propositions: claims written down in advance so that they can be tested. That is slower, but more honest. For each claim, the ledger says what process is being tested, which measures would matter, and what finding would force a rethink.

Sources L001 L002 L019 L022 S025 S027 S028

The seven propositions are not verdicts. P01 through P07 are working claims about access, corporate sponsorship, platform entry, songs crossing regions and languages, the reach of Pakistanis abroad, the prestige of international recognition, and the uneven way visibility turns into a living. The ledger below comes straight from the project’s proposition table rather than being rewritten as persuasive prose. That matters because every row carries its own mechanism, main measures, evidence still needed, rule for revision, and status before fieldwork. This text can explain the ledger. It should not sneak in a stronger conclusion than the table allows.

Package references proposition_matrix.csv analysis_plan.md

The revision rules are what most directly stop the project from seeing only what it hopes to see. If similar songs with no institutional backing reach audiences at similar rates, the platform-entry claim weakens. If labels without brand money, public funds, venues, and independent income keep careers going just as well, the sponsorship claim must change. If under-represented artists get similar ownership, pay, continuity, and say over decisions once career stage is taken into account, the social-diversity claim cannot stay as it is. These are not decorative caveats. They are the conditions under which the argument would lose.

Package references proposition_matrix.csv analysis_plan.md

Read this way, Side B is not asking whether Pakistani music rose. It asks which part of the system moved, who could turn that movement into rights or income, and where the public record stops. The ledger turns optimism into duties that could prove the project wrong. Collect the measures, look for cases that break the pattern, keep platform attention apart from institutional strength, and leave unmeasured value blank until the evidence exists. It also makes later corrections a normal part of the work rather than an embarrassment.

Side B — Value

05 Comparative pathways

The cases are not trophies. They are evidence of routes: documented sequences through which artists, collectives, middlemen, platforms, brands, and foreign institutions made particular careers visible. The comparison asks three things of each route: what it shows about how the music spread, what it cannot show about rights or payment, and where one success story would mislead if it were treated as a national pattern. The same rule applies to all seven cases. Visibility counts only where the listed sources document it. Income, ownership, contract terms, and how typical a case is all remain unknown unless a source actually provides them.

Side B — Value

06 Methods in detail

The methods keep six questions apart: what music gets made, who listens, whether musicians can make a living, how mature the industry’s institutions are, who is included, and how far the music reaches abroad. The planned 360-track sample, 93 track variables, and 51 artist variables are research targets, not findings. They set out what must be observed before the propositions can be tested with more confidence. Tracks are chosen on purpose rather than at random, because the study needs variety across years, languages, genres, regions, gender, routes into the industry, and cases that break the pattern. That design lets the study compare how things work. It does not turn 30 tracks a year into a statistical portrait of every Pakistani release or every scene.

The interviews aim for 38 people: artists, producers, middlemen, brands or platforms, people in live music, rights and policy specialists, journalists, and fans. The interview guide asks about the cost of getting started, income ranges, late payments, contracts, platform data, how brands choose artists, live work, class, gender, language, region, the diaspora, censorship, and what people mean by an industry. Side B — Value

07 Rights, policy, and the missing ledger

Rights have to be pulled apart before they can be judged. First, there are rights in law: Pakistan has a copyright law that recognizes songs, recordings, ownership, transfers of ownership, performance, broadcasting, and related categories. Second, there is rights administration: the practical systems that register works, attach the right details to them, identify owners, license uses, collect money, and settle disputes. Third come contracts, because a law does not tell us whether a performer kept ownership of a recording, signed away a song, accepted a flat fee, shared royalties, or understood what had to be earned back first. Fourth and fifth come collecting the money and actually paying it out. A right that exists on paper can still fail to become money in an artist’s account.

Sources S012 S018 L016 L018

The National Music Policy belongs in that layered reading. The version on the government’s website is a final draft. Its own agenda names problems with copyright, piracy, archives, licensing, welfare, education, enforcement, and institutional support. Earlier official statements announced a policy process, while a later outside review of Pakistan’s intellectual-property system still described the policy as a draft. Together, that supports a cautious claim: the record shows that the government recognizes gaps in the music sector and has proposed fixes, not that anything has been put into practice. It does not show that the policy took effect, was funded, was enforced, collected money, or changed anything for artists. The policy page below can quote and organize the draft’s stated goals, but it cannot treat those goals as proof that a registry, welfare scheme, rights body, or payment channel started working.

Sources L016 L017 L018 S013 S014

This matters because in the streaming era, fame often arrives before the paperwork works. A song can spread on YouTube or Spotify, appear in a branded programme, get press, or enter a foreign institution while the public still cannot see who holds its rights. The public record may show a platform announcement, a programme credit, or an award. It usually does not show the contract that decided ownership, the statement that calculated royalties, the payment date, or how a dispute would be settled if payment failed. It rarely tells us whether artists got statements they could understand, or only a few selected campaign numbers. Treating the visible moment as proof of a working rights economy confuses a song’s release with the chain that turns it into income.

The missing ledger is a research result of a different kind: not a number, but a boundary. This package holds no public national data for Pakistan on recorded-music revenue, artist royalty payments, music employment, how common contracts are, how many venues exist, late payments, songwriting royalties collected, or who owns recordings. Global music reports can describe international markets, but their figures cannot be applied to Pakistan without evidence from Pakistan. The BNU/UNESCO survey can warn about the livelihoods of the people it chose to survey, but it cannot become a national wage or employment series. The honest way to chart this is labelled blank space, not zeroes, grey placeholder bars, or borrowed global estimates that would only disguise what is missing.

Chapter 07 is where the title stops being a metaphor. Virality can be seen from the outside: charts, campaigns, platforms’ own announcements, press profiles, and awards all leave traces. An industry, in the fuller sense, needs systems that are harder to see: ownership that can be enforced, contracts people can understand, routine collection of royalties, reliable payment, venues, training, archives, and a way to settle disputes. The draft policy may name many of those needs, and the law may define the rights. The open question is whether artists can actually turn visibility into bargaining power and pay, through institutions that work for ordinary musicians and leave public evidence behind.
